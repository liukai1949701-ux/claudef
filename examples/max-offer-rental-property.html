How to calculate your max offer on a rental property

Instead of asking whether a listing works at the asking price, work backwards: choose the returns you need and solve for the highest price that still delivers them. Here is the method on one example duplex, with every formula and number shown.

DoorMath · Published September 27, 2026 · All figures are for a made-up example property, not a real listing or market data.

The example deal

A duplex listed at $239,000. Both units together rent for $2,500 a month, plus $60 of other income. The buyer puts 25% down on a 30-year loan at 7.00%, pays 3% closing costs and spends $18,000 on repairs at purchase.

Inputs
Units2
Asking price$239,000
Rent (both units) + other income$2,500 + $60 a month
Property taxes · insurance$3,300 · $1,450 a year
Utilities & other owner-paid costs$200 a month
Vacancy · maintenance · CapEx reserve6% · 5% · 5% of gross income
Property management8% of collected income
Loan25% down, 7.00%, 30 years, no points
Closing costs · up-front repairs3% of the price · $18,000 in cash

The buyer's targets are at least $150 a month of cash flow per door, an 8% cash-on-cash return, a 6% cap rate and a DSCR of 1.25.

At the asking price the duplex makes $169 a month ($85 per door), a 2.4% cash-on-cash return, a 6.8% cap rate and a DSCR of 1.14. Only the cap-rate target passes. So what price would pass all four?

Step 1: work out NOI, which doesn't depend on the price

Net operating income (NOI) is what the property earns before the mortgage. In this budget none of the lines depends on the price, so NOI stays fixed while we search for the price.

Monthly NOI
Rent + other income$2,560.00
Vacancy (6% of gross)−$153.60
Collected income$2,406.40
Management (8% of collected)−$192.51
Maintenance (5% of gross)−$128.00
CapEx reserve (5% of gross)−$128.00
Property taxes ($3,300 ÷ 12)−$275.00
Insurance ($1,450 ÷ 12)−$120.83
Utilities & other−$200.00
NOI$1,362.05

That is $16,344.66 a year.

Property taxes are held at $3,300 a year. If your county reassesses at the purchase price, a lower price also lowers the taxes, so your true max offer is a little higher.

Step 2: turn the loan into a cost per dollar of price

The monthly payment on a fixed-rate loan is the loan amount times a payment factor k:

k = i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n)      i = 7.00% ÷ 12,  n = 360 payments
k = 0.0066530

The loan is 75% of the price, so each dollar of price adds 0.75 × 0.0066530 = 0.0049898 to the monthly payment, about $4.99 a month for every $1,000 of price. The cash you put in is 28% of the price (25% down plus 3% closing costs) plus the $18,000 of repairs.

Step 3: solve each target for the price

Each metric is a simple function of the price P, so each target turns into a maximum price. With NOI = $1,362.05 a month:

TargetSolve for the priceMax price
Cash flow ≥ $150 per door(NOI − 2 × $150) ÷ 0.0049898$212,846
Cash-on-cash ≥ 8%(12 × NOI − 8% × $18,000) ÷ (12 × 0.0049898 + 8% × 28%)$181,152
Cap rate ≥ 6%12 × NOI ÷ 6%$272,411
DSCR ≥ 1.25NOI ÷ (1.25 × 0.0049898)$218,376

Cash-on-cash is the only line that needs a little algebra, because the price is on both sides: a lower price lowers the payment and the cash you put in.

annual cash flow ÷ cash invested  ≥  8%
12 × (NOI − 0.0049898 × P)  ≥  8% × (0.28 × P + $18,000)
$16,344.66 − 0.0598772 × P  ≥  0.0224 × P + $1,440
P  ≤  $14,904.66 ÷ 0.0822772  =  $181,152

Max offer: $181,152. It is the lowest of the four prices, so it is the only one that meets every target. Here the cash-on-cash target is the one that binds. That is $57,848 (24%) below the asking price.

Step 4: check the deal at that price

At $181,152 the loan is $135,864 and the payment is $903.90 a month. Everything else is unchanged.

  • $458cash flow a month ($229 per door)
  • 8.0%cash-on-cash (target 8%)
  • 9.0%cap rate (target 6%)
  • 1.51DSCR (target 1.25)
  • $68,722cash invested

At each of the other three prices, that target's own metric lands exactly on target: $150.00 per door at $212,846, a 6.00% cap rate at $272,411 and a DSCR of 1.25 at $218,376. All three prices are still too high for the 8% cash-on-cash target.

What moves the max offer

Changing one input at a time, starting from the $181,152 result:

ChangeMax offerDifference
Interest rate 6.00% instead of 7.00%$195,190+$14,039
Rent $2,700 instead of $2,500$203,461+$22,309
Self-managed (0% management instead of 8%)$209,229+$28,078
30% down instead of 25%$181,134−$18

A bigger down payment barely moves an offer that cash-on-cash is limiting: each extra dollar down lowers the payment, but it also adds a dollar to the cash invested. Rent, management and the interest rate move it far more.

Where the 1% rule and the 70% rule fit

The 1% rule says monthly rent should be at least 1% of the price plus repairs. Solved for the price: $2,500 × 100 − $18,000 = $232,000. It is a quick screen, not a target: here it would let you pay $50,848 more than the 8% cash-on-cash target allows.

The 70% rule is for flips: pay at most 70% of the after-repair value, minus repairs. With an after-repair value of $285,000 that is $181,500, close to $181,152 only by coincidence. It answers a different question (flip profit), so don't use it to price a rental.

What this leaves out

  • Income taxes and depreciation. All figures are before tax.
  • Appreciation and loan paydown. They add to your total return but not to monthly cash flow.
  • Costs that change with the price. Taxes and insurance are held fixed here (see the note in step 1).
