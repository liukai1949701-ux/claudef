Cash-on-cash vs. cap rate vs. DSCR: one duplex, three answers

Cap rate, cash-on-cash return and DSCR all describe the same rental, yet for this example duplex they read 6.84%, 2.40% and 1.14. They disagree because they answer different questions. Here is what each one measures and when to use it.

DoorMath · Published September 27, 2026 · All figures are for a made-up example property, not a real listing or market data.

Three metrics, three questions

For the example duplex: $239,000, 25% down on a 30-year loan at 7.00%, NOI of $16,345 a year and loan payments of $14,311 a year.

MetricFormulaThis duplex
Cap rateNOI ÷ price$16,345 ÷ $239,000 = 6.84%
Cash-on-cash returncash flow ÷ cash invested$2,034 ÷ $84,920 = 2.40%
DSCRNOI ÷ loan payments$16,345 ÷ $14,311 = 1.14
  • Cap rate asks what the property earns on its price before any loan. Because it ignores financing, it's the one to compare across properties and against sale prices in the area.
  • Cash-on-cash return asks what your cash earns in the first year. The cash invested here is the down payment, closing costs and up-front repairs. It ignores loan paydown and appreciation.
  • DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) asks whether NOI covers the loan payments, and by how much. It is what lenders check, and each lender sets its own minimum. Below 1.00, NOI doesn't cover the payments.

The cap rate moves only with the price or NOI. Financing changes cash-on-cash and DSCR but never the cap rate. $181,152 is the price that makes this duplex hit an 8% cash-on-cash return; the max-offer example shows how to solve for it.

Why a 6.84% cap rate can mean a 2.40% return

Bought with cash, the duplex returns 6.19% on its $264,170 all-in cost (price, closing costs and repairs). The loan changes that because of its loan constant, the yearly payment per dollar borrowed:

loan constant = 12 × monthly payment ÷ loan amount
at 7.00% for 30 years: 12 × 0.0066530 = 7.98% a year

Each borrowed dollar costs 7.98% a year in payments, but the property earns only 6.19% on it. So borrowing pulls the return down from 6.19% all cash to 2.40% with 75% financing. This is called negative leverage. At 6.00% the constant is 7.19%, still too high; for this deal a loan only starts to raise the cash-on-cash return below a rate of about 4.65%.

Part of each loan payment is principal, which builds equity rather than disappearing. Cash-on-cash leaves that out, so it understates the total return of a financed purchase.

At the $239,000 asking price, with the same loan and expense budget, the rent for both units would need to be at least:

With this budget each extra dollar of rent adds about 76 cents of NOI: vacancy, management, maintenance and CapEx take the rest. The DSCR line, for example:

NOI needed = 1.25 × $1,192.55 = $1,490.69 a month
NOI        = 0.7648 × (rent + $60) − $595.83 fixed costs
rent       = ($1,490.69 + $595.83) ÷ 0.7648 − $60 = $2,668.20

Which one to use

  • Screening listings, or checking a price against local sales: cap rate.
  • Deciding whether your cash is better here than elsewhere: cash-on-cash, plus loan paydown and appreciation for the full picture.
  • Getting a loan, or stress-testing a vacancy or a rate rise: DSCR.
  • Making an offer: all of them at once. Solve each target for a price and offer the lowest, as in the max-offer example.
