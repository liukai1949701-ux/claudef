BRRRR worked example: do you get all your cash back?

BRRRR (buy, rehab, rent, refinance, repeat) works when the refinance pays back the cash you put in and the rent still covers the new loan. Those are two separate tests, and the price you pay only affects one of them. Here are two deals run through the same financing.

DoorMath · Published September 27, 2026 · All figures are for a made-up example property, not a real listing or market data.

The two deals

Both deals use the same short-term loan and the same refinance. Deal A is the example duplex from the free calculator, bought close to market value. Deal B is bought at a bigger discount and needs more work.

InputDeal ADeal B
Purchase price$239,000$170,000
Rehab$18,000$30,000
After-repair value (ARV)$285,000$300,000
Rent (both units) + other income$2,500 + $60$3,100 + $60
Property taxes · insurance a year$3,300 · $1,450$3,900 · $1,650

Shared assumptions:

  • Short-term (hard-money) loan: 90% of the price plus 100% of the rehab, 11% interest-only, 2 points.
  • Rehab period: 4 months, with $450 a month of holding costs while the property is empty.
  • Buyer closing costs: 3% of the price.
  • Refinance: 75% of ARV on a 30-year loan at 7.25%, with refinance costs of 2% of the new loan.
  • Operating budget once rented: vacancy 6%, maintenance 5% and CapEx 5% of gross income, management 8% of collected income, utilities and other costs $200 a month.

Deal A's new loan doesn't even pay off the short-term loan, so the buyer brings another $23,625 to the refinance closing and ends up with $69,704 stuck in the deal. Deal B's refinance pays back all $34,270 the buyer put in, plus $3,230.

Test 1: do you get your cash back?

Put steps 1 and 2 together and everything collapses into one comparison:

cash left in the deal = all-in cost − (new loan − refinance costs)
                      = all-in cost − 75% × (1 − 2%) × ARV
                      = all-in cost − 73.5% × ARV

With this financing you get all your cash back only when the all-in cost is no more than 73.5% of ARV. Deal A is at 98%; Deal B is at 72%.

Deal A loses $1,153 a year on top of the $69,704 left in it. Deal B has no cash left in the deal, so its cash-on-cash return is undefined (the "infinite return" people talk about); judge it by the $219 a month of cash flow and the $75,000 of equity instead.

Test 2: does the rent carry the new loan?

The new loan is sized from the ARV, not from what you paid. So cash flow after the refinance depends on the rent compared with the ARV, and the purchase price has no effect on it.

Buying Deal A for less fixes Test 1 but not Test 2. At a price of $174,518 or less, Deal A would return all its cash, but the new loan would still be $213,750 and the duplex would still lose $96 a month. To break even after the refinance it needs rent of about $2,626 a month instead of $2,500.

75% vs. 70% loan-to-value

Some lenders cap a cash-out refinance at 70% of the appraised value. The smaller loan leaves more cash in the deal but lowers the payment:

At 70% the all-cash-back threshold drops from 73.5% to 68.6% of ARV, so Deal B would leave $11,470 in.

What can go wrong

  • The appraisal. The refinance is based on the appraised value, not your ARV estimate. If Deal B appraised at $280,000 instead of $300,000, it would leave $11,470 in the deal instead of none.
  • Waiting periods. Some lenders want you to have owned the property for several months before they lend on the new value. Each extra month on Deal B's short-term loan costs about $1,678 of interest plus $450 of holding costs.
  • Rehab overruns add straight to the all-in cost, and so to the cash left in the deal.
  • The refinance rate changes the new payment and the cash flow, but not the cash left in the deal.
